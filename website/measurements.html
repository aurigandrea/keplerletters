Network Analysis

What can we learn from measuring the Kepler letters?

Using graph-theoretic measures on the directed weighted network of Kepler's correspondence, we can quantify influence, brokerage, and structural importance. These charts explore who held power in the network, what changed when Kepler is removed, and which place-year combinations stand out as unexpected hubs of activity. However, this reveals as much about the archival collection itself as about the Kepler's social world. Use them as indicators leading to potential stories hidden in the content of the letters.

The popularity contest

Who were the most influential, most active, and most connected people in Kepler's correspondence?

Top 25 Correspondents by Centrality Measure

Switch between metrics to compare how rankings shift depending on what kind of influence you measure. A quick catch-up on terminology: PageRank weights incoming letters by the sender's own importance — a letter from Galileo counts more than a letter from an unknown clerk. Betweenness captures who sits on the most shortest paths between others, they are the network brokers. Degree in this case simply counts total letter volume. Toggle Kepler removed to see the underlying network without him dominating every metric.

Degree, how many letters were exchanged, is a meaningful indicator of someone's importance in Kepler's connections. As this is an ego-centric network, of course, it's concentrated around Kepler. If we remove him, we naturally loose him as a broker, but also we can explore how influence is redistributed across institutional and scholarly actors (for example Tübingen University, Mästlin, Brahe, and Galileo), indicating that many secondary ties were previously masked by Kepler's hub effect. Read this chart together with betweenness: actors with moderate PageRank but non-zero betweenness are often the most meaningful brokers in the residual network.

What changes without Kepler?

Removing Kepler from the graph reveals the underlying structure of his correspondents' own connections.

Betweenness Centrality: Full Network vs. Kepler Removed

As above, when Kepler is removed, it's expected that his correspondents lose nearly all betweenness — their brokerage role was entirely mediated through him. A few figures, however, maintain independent structural positions in the residual network. PageRank gains (shown as the teal bars) reveal who becomes more prominent in Kepler's absence. It's the historian's task to explore if it's a collection error or there is a meaningul reason to include those letters in the Kepler archive.

Brahe and Galileo lose the most betweenness when Kepler is removed — they were connected to the wider network almost exclusively through him. It doesn't tell much about their overall influence, but highlights Kepler's connection to them. In contrast, Tübingen University and Cysat show PageRank gains, suggesting they hold independent connections not reliant on Kepler as a hub. When you find someoone who unexpectedly holds a bridge in the residual network, it's a strong signal to go back to the archives.

The promising outliers

Where and when were highly-connected people present in otherwise peripheral locations?

Top 30 Unexpected Places of Activity

Each bubble represents a place in a given year. The vertical position shows the brokerage intensity score (high-centrality people in low-activity places), bubble size encodes the number of letter mentions, and colour encodes the number of distinct people present. Hover for full details. Toggle Kepler removed to see what remains in the secondary network.

When Kepler is in the network, Prague (1601–1606) and Graz (1594–1597) dominate as high-intensity hubs. It's not a surprise as Kepler was resident in these cities and drew significant correspondence. One-off locations like Baierdorf and Steyregg score exceptionally high because they hosted single letters from/to Kepler despite minimal background activity. Without Kepler: Cities like Tübingen, Strasbourg, and Munich emerge as more persistent secondary hubs for correspondence, revealing the role of institutional networks in Kepler's circles. What's worth exploring is the small places with high brokerage scores — they may indicate unexpected encounters or secret journeys. That's where network science ends, and microhistory begins.
